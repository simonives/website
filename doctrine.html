Approach

How I think about the work.

These three practices are not a method I apply to a problem from the outside. They are the disposition I bring to it: the questions I cannot help asking, the measure I return to, and the sense of timing that governing at scale forces on you. They emerged from the work. They are not a menu.

  1. 01 Socratic
  2. 02 Utilitarian
  3. 03 Polyrhythm

The Socratic practice

Every problem arrives pre-described. Someone has already decided what it is before anyone asks whether that description is true. The first move is to refuse the given framing and find the actual problem underneath it, which is almost never the one on the slide. Most failed transformations solved a problem no one had.

The stated problem is a claim, not a fact. Treat it as one.

The utilitarian practice

The measure of a system is the aggregate value it returns to the people inside it. Friction is waste, and waste has a moral weight when it is paid in the hours of a global workforce. A system that saves the enterprise money and costs its people their attention has not been made better. It has moved the cost somewhere harder to see.

The polyrhythm of transformation

A complex enterprise never changes at one tempo. Technology deploys in quarters. Behaviour shifts over years. Capital releases on its own cycle, indifferent to both. Governing change means holding these rhythms at once and refusing to pretend they are the same beat. Force them into unison and something breaks: the rollout that arrives before anyone is ready, the funding that lands after the moment has passed.

This is the practice least visible from outside the work, and the one that decides whether a programme survives contact with a real organisation.

Practical wisdom for enterprise transformation.
